Technology

How a block gets made.

From sourcing to curing, plus the performance data — labeled by what's measured, what's a target, and what's still to be confirmed.

Sourcing

Two delivery routes, no extra trucks.

Cement and limestone dust both come from Garadagh and travel together on one truck. Plastic bales and incinerator ash both come from Temiz Sheher's Balakhani site and travel together on a second. Microsilica, CO2 gas, and cellulose pulp are all sourced locally within Sumgait.

The waste-to-energy plant would otherwise pay to landfill the ash it hands us — supplying it to us instead saves that cost, at no charge to us. Plastic arrives as pre-sorted bales; we sort further by resin code and reject anything that isn't clean HDPE or PP.

Process

Four steps, in order.

01

Sort & shred

Plastic is dry visual-sorted by resin code — HDPE and PP only, no water tank, since wet plastic throws off the mix — then shredded. Incinerator ash is stockpiled outdoors on a sealed pad for a minimum of eight weeks before use.

02

Mix

Sorted plastic, weathered ash, limestone dust, cement, and small amounts of microsilica and cellulose pulp are combined in a pan mixer.

03

Press

The mix is pressed into a two-core hollow steel mold at full block scale, 390 × 190 × 190 mm.

04

Cure

Instead of curing in water, the pressed block sits in a sealed chamber under CO2 gas for 24 hours. The gas reacts with calcium in the cement and mineralizes into solid calcium carbonate inside the block.

Why the ash gets weathered first. Fresh incinerator ash contains small amounts of metallic aluminum, which reacts with the alkaline cement paste and can crack a block days after pressing. Every batch is weathered outdoors and checked — a visual inspection plus a bubble test — before it's used.

Front face, hollow CMU — to scale

Geometry

Why it's hollow.

Solid, at this density, the block would weigh roughly 25 kg — heavier than the standard stone block it's meant to replace. Two through-cores bring the calculated weight down to about 12.3 kg, roughly a 50% void ratio, while keeping a face shell of at least 32 mm on every wall.

How it works

Five mechanisms, explained plainly.

Mechanical interlock

The angular shape of the shredded plastic gets gripped by the hardened cement around it, similar to how gravel locks into ordinary concrete. Microsilica helps the cement bond more tightly to the plastic surface. Zhang et al., 2023

CO2 curing

Instead of a water cure, the pressed block sits in a CO2 atmosphere. The gas reacts with calcium hydroxide in the cement and mineralizes into solid calcium carbonate — the same mineral limestone is made of. Tu et al., 2018

Fire behavior

The ash is inert and pre-burned at roughly 1,000°C, and the plastic sits encased in a mineral matrix with limited oxygen. We're targeting EN 13501-1 Class B, but haven't tested for it yet.

Sound

Cement and plastic transmit sound differently, which should reduce how much passes through the wall. This hasn't been measured yet.

Self-healing

The mix carries more cement than fully reacts during curing. Moisture reaching a small crack later can trigger new mineral growth that seals it — a mechanism documented in published research on similar concretes. We haven't tested it on our own blocks yet. Lahmann et al., 2023

What we ruled out

Not every waste stream made the cut.

LDPE soft film

Flat, thin flakes that clump together in the mix and don't get coated properly by the cement paste.

Mixed cable scrap

Real-world cable scrap usually still carries PVC jacketing, which releases hydrogen chloride gas if it ever burns. Only cleanly stripped insulation would be safe, and we can't verify that without certification.

Cement kiln dust

Can carry trace heavy metals and would need leaching tests before use. Left out for now to keep the process simple.

PVC, in any form

Not accepted in any feedstock, full stop.
